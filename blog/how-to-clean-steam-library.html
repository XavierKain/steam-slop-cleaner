If you've been on Steam for more than a few years, chances are your library has become a sprawling mess. Between Humble Bundle deals, free-to-keep promotions, and late-night impulse buys during sales, the average Steam user has accumulated dozens — sometimes hundreds — of games they'll never play. Many of those games aren't just unwanted; they're genuinely low-quality titles that clutter your experience every time you open the client.

This guide walks you through every method available to clean up your Steam library in 2025, from basic organization to permanently removing games you never asked for. By the end, you'll have a streamlined collection that only shows the games you actually care about.

Why You Should Clean Your Steam Library

A cluttered Steam library isn't just an aesthetic problem — it actively makes your gaming experience worse. When you have 400 games and only play 20 of them, finding what you want becomes a chore. The psychological effect is real too: a bloated library creates decision paralysis, and studies show that having too many choices leads to less satisfaction with the choices you make.

There's also the practical side. A cleaner library means faster load times in the Steam client, easier navigation on the Steam Deck, and a much better experience when browsing on mobile. If you use Steam's family sharing features, a clean library means your family members aren't wading through shovelware to find the games worth playing.

Beyond personal comfort, there's a community angle. When you share your Steam profile or compare libraries with friends, a curated collection says something about your taste. Nobody wants to explain why they own "Hentai Puzzle 7" alongside their carefully chosen indie collection.

Step 1: Audit Your Collection

Before you start organizing, you need to understand what you're working with. Open Steam and navigate to your library. Switch to the list view (the icon in the top-right of the library panel) so you can see everything at a glance. Take note of the total number of games — this is your starting point.

Now, sort your library by "Recent Activity." Games you haven't touched in over a year are prime candidates for cleanup. Next, sort by "Name" and scroll through slowly. You'll be surprised how many games you don't even recognize. Those mystery titles? Usually free-to-play games you tried once, shovelware from bundles, or promotional items tied to events long past.

What Counts as "Clutter"?

Not every game you don't play is clutter. A game you finished and loved? That's a trophy. A game you haven't started but genuinely want to play? That's your backlog. Clutter is the rest: games you'll never play, games you didn't choose, games that aren't even really games. Be honest with yourself during this audit — if you haven't touched it in two years, you're probably not going to.

Step 2: Set Up Categories and Collections

Steam's built-in organization tools have improved significantly over the past few years. The two main systems are Categories (manual folders) and Collections (dynamic or manual groups visible in the sidebar).

Right-click any game and select "Manage > Add to > New Collection." Create collections that match how you actually think about your games. Here's a structure that works well for most people:

  • Currently Playing — Games you're actively working through
  • Up Next — Your curated short list of what to play next
  • Backlog — Games you own and want to play eventually
  • Completed — Games you've finished
  • Multiplayer — Games you play with friends
  • Hidden — Games you want to keep but not see

Dynamic collections are even more powerful. You can create collections that automatically filter by tags, genres, or even Steam features (like controller support or Steam Deck compatibility). For example, create a dynamic collection for "Steam Deck Verified" games, and it'll update automatically as Valve verifies more titles.

Pro Tip: The Favorites Shelf

Pin your "Currently Playing" collection to the top of your library as a shelf. This means the games you actually want to play are always front and center, regardless of how many hundreds of titles lurk beneath. To do this, go to your library home, click the small gear icon near the top, and select "Add Shelf" then choose your collection.

Step 3: Hide Games You Don't Want to See

For games you own but never want to see again, Steam's "Hide" feature is your best friend. Right-click any game, go to "Manage," and click "Hide this game." The game disappears from your main library view entirely. It's still in your account — you just don't have to look at it.

Hidden games live in a special "Hidden" collection accessible from your library sidebar. You can unhide them anytime if you change your mind. This is the ideal solution for bundle shovelware, promotional items, and free games you claimed but have zero interest in.

A word of caution: hiding games doesn't remove them from your game count on your profile. If you care about that number (and many collectors do), you'll need to actually remove games, which we cover in the next section.

Step 4: Permanently Remove Games

Yes, you can actually remove games from your Steam account permanently. The process depends on whether the game was free or purchased.

For free games: Go to the Steam Support page (help.steampowered.com), click "Games, Software, etc.," find the game, and select "I want to permanently remove this game from my account." It's instant and irreversible.

For purchased games: You can request a refund if the game is within the refund window (under 2 hours played, within 14 days of purchase). For games outside the refund window, you can still permanently remove them through the same Support page, but you won't get your money back. Think of it as paying for a cleaner library.

Before removing anything, consider whether you might want the game later. Steam occasionally updates games, adds new content, or fixes major issues. A game that's terrible today might be decent in a year. When in doubt, hide instead of remove.

Step 6: Maintain Your Clean Library

Cleaning your library once is great. Keeping it clean is the real challenge. Here are habits that prevent clutter from building up again:

Be selective with bundles. Just because a bundle has one game you want doesn't mean you need to redeem every key. Many bundle sites let you choose which keys to activate. Leave the rest.

Skip the "free to keep" traps. When Steam or other platforms offer free games, ask yourself: would I pay even $1 for this? If not, don't add it to your library. Your time scrolling past it in the future costs more than the game is worth.

Do a quarterly cleanup. Set a reminder every three months to spend 15 minutes hiding or removing games that slipped through. It's much easier to maintain a clean library than to overhaul a messy one.

Use wishlists properly. Instead of buying games on sale "just in case," add them to your wishlist. Steam will notify you when they go on sale again. The game isn't going anywhere.

Advanced Tips for Power Users

If you're serious about library management, these techniques go beyond the basics:

Custom Categories with Tagging

Steam allows you to add personal tags to games. Use these to create a tagging system that works for your workflow. For example, tag games as "10min-sessions," "weekend-games," or "podcast-friendly" based on how you actually play.

Steam Deck Optimization

If you own a Steam Deck, create a dynamic collection filtered by "Steam Deck Verified" and "Steam Deck Playable." This gives you an instant list of games that'll work perfectly on the handheld without any tinkering.

Export Your Library Data

Tools like SteamDB and Steam Slop Cleaner let you export your library data as CSV files. This is useful for tracking your collection over time, comparing with friends, or making bulk decisions about what to keep.

Use Multiple Library Views

Don't stick with just one view. Use the grid view for browsing (the cover art helps jog your memory), list view for organizing (you can see everything at once), and the detail view for individual games you're evaluating. Switch between them depending on what you're doing.

Conclusion

A clean Steam library isn't about having fewer games — it's about having a collection that reflects what you actually enjoy. The process doesn't need to happen all at once. Start by hiding the obvious junk, set up a few collections, and let tools like Steam Slop Cleaner handle the heavy lifting of identifying low-quality titles.

The payoff is real: less decision paralysis, faster game launching, and the satisfaction of a library that feels intentional rather than accidental. Your future self — the one who just wants to play something good after a long day — will thank you.
